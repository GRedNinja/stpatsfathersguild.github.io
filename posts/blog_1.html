On Fatherhood

Topic: What Does It Mean to Be a Father?

What does it mean to be a father? What is it that a father does? If tradition is a form of practical reasoning, then we should not begin by asking what a father is in the abstract. We should ask what the Western tradition has recognized as exemplary fatherhood.

Method

Tradition supplies a body of human experience against which our own judgments can be tested. Fatherhood could be understood biologically, legally, or in terms of personal preference, contemporary cultural norms, sociology, or government policy. None of these can answer in a satisfactory way the teleological imperative “What is a father for?” nor can they answer “What ought a father to do?”

Tradition provides data for analysis in a multitude of ways. For our purposes, we examine the Western tradition of fatherhood through classical literature. Humans gain knowledge through practice, example, and case studies. Literature provides case studies within a moral context:

  • Hector was a father. Hector did X. It is up to the reader to discern whether Hector was right or wrong.

These cases are often received and reinterpreted by future authors. Homer tells us about fatherhood via Hector. Virgil inherits Homer and provides another view of fatherhood via Aeneas. Dante receives both Homer and Virgil and gives us more data to examine. The Holy Gospel presents St. Joseph as the exemplar of paternal devotion.

Points of Analysis

  • Homer’s Hector
  • Virgil’s Aeneas
  • Dante’s treatment of fatherhood in the Divine Comedy
  • St. Joseph in the Holy Gospel
