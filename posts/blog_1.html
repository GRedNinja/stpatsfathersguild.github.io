De Paternitate — On Fatherhood

(Prepared as a Jesuit-style lecture script for publication as a blog essay)

I. Opening: The Question of Fatherhood

Today we take up a question that is as old as civilization and as urgent as the present hour: What does it mean to be a father, and what is it that a father ought to do?

Modern discourse offers many answers. Fatherhood can be described biologically, legally, culturally, sociologically, or even in terms of government policy. Yet none of these frameworks can answer the deeper, teleological question:

What is a father for?
What ought a father to do?

These approaches describe functions, but not ends. They tell us what fathers are, but not what fathers are for.

II. Method: Tradition as Practical Reasoning

Tradition is a form of practical reasoning and practical reasoning begins not with abstraction but with exemplars. We do not begin by asking, “What is a father in theory?” We begin by asking:

What has the Western tradition recognized as exemplary fatherhood?

Tradition is not nostalgia. It is a body of tested human experience, a treasury of moral data against which our own judgments may be measured.

And among the richest sources of this data is classical literature.

Literature gives us case studies—moral examples embodied in characters whose actions can be judged. Hector was a father. Hector acted. The reader must discern whether Hector acted well or poorly.

These exempla do not stand alone. They are received, critiqued, and developed by later authors. Homer gives us Hector. Virgil receives Homer and gives us Aeneas. Dante receives both and gives us a Christianized vision of paternal formation. The Gospel then elevates fatherhood beyond nature through St. Joseph.

Thus emerges a continuous tradition of paternal exemplarity.

III. Case Studies in the Western Tradition

1. Homer’s Hector: Duty and Tragedy

Hector stands in contrast to Achilles. Achilles lives for his own pride; Hector lives for those entrusted to him.

In Iliad VI.440–493, Hector prays that his son may surpass him in virtue. He desires not his own glory, but his son’s flourishing.

Yet Hector dies and fails to save Troy. His fatherhood is marked by courage, sacrifice, and tragic limitation. He shows us that fatherhood is not measured by worldly success but by fidelity to one’s charge.

2. Virgil’s Aeneas: Transmission and Foundation

Virgil receives Homer and deepens the theme.

In Aeneid II, Aeneas becomes a bridge between generations: Aeneas carries his father Anchises and leads his son by the hand; Anchises carries the household gods. This image is hierarchical, symbolic, and theological.

In Aeneid VI, Aeneas seeks his father in death. Anchises continues to be a father even beyond the grave. Aeneas becomes the link between the dead, the living, and the unborn.

Fatherhood here is civilizational.the transmission of culture, faith, and identity.

3. St. Joseph: Obedience and Divine Alignment

The Gospel then reveals fatherhood apart from biology.

St. Joseph is father not by nature but by vocation. His fatherhood is defined by uniformity to the will of God. He is an earthly icon of divine care.

Joseph has no recorded words in Scripture. His fatherhood is expressed entirely through action, obedience, and custodianship.

He shows that fatherhood is not merely natural or cultural,it is spiritual, a participation in divine providence.

IV. Synthesis: What the Tradition Teaches

Across these exempla, a pattern emerges:

Hector teaches paternal courage and sacrificial duty.
Aeneas teaches paternal transmission and civilizational continuity.
St. Joseph teaches paternal obedience and divine alignment.

The Western tradition consistently binds fatherhood to inheritance not primarily monetary, but religious, symbolic, cultural, and moral.

The Gospel elevates this inheritance further, presenting fatherhood as a participation in the care of God Himself.

V. Conclusion: The Teleology of Fatherhood

Thus we may conclude:

A father is ordered toward the protection, formation, and transmission of the temporal, cultural, and spiritual good.

Fatherhood is not a biological accident nor a legal category. It is a vocation. A father is the guardian of memory, the bridge between generations, the custodian of the household, and the earthly sign of divine care.

This is the vision of fatherhood the Western tradition gives us. This is the vision the Gospel perfects.
