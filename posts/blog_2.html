Lecture Outline: On a Good Inheritance

I. Central Question: What Is an Inheritance?

The practical end of fatherhood is inheritance: every father leaves one behind, whether he intends to or not. Some inherit piety, others inherit vice, disordered habits, or fractured social relations. Therefore, the first duty of a father is to understand what an inheritance is, so that he may avoid transmitting a corrupt one and instead build a worthy patrimony.

II. The Constituent Parts of an Inheritance

  • Literary witness: In Western literary examples, money has almost no place in inheritance. In the Iliad, the Aeneid, and in the Gospel account of St. Joseph, the inheritance is honor, mission, guardianship, and fidelity—not cash.
  • Bourdieu’s schema: Economic, cultural, social, and symbolic capital form the natural structure of human inheritance.
  • Religious capital: The Western tradition also explicitly includes religious capital: the inheritance of faith, moral formation, and right living.

III. Explanation of the Types of Capital

1. Economic Capital – What You Own

Tools, land, businesses, and other productive assets that allow a family to sustain itself and serve others.

2. Social Capital – Who You Know and Who Knows You

Family relations, friendships, reputation, and professional ties that create a network of trust and mutual aid.

3. Cultural Capital – What You Know and Can Do

Education, books, manners, traditions, and craftsmanship: the skills and habits that shape how a family lives and thinks.

4. Symbolic Capital – What Signifies Your Lineage

Family heirlooms, stories, photographs, and symbols that give a sense of identity and continuity across generations.

5. Religious Capital – Knowledge of God and Right Living

Religious capital does not necessarily translate into earthly success. Yet it goes before all other types of wealth because it supplies the answers to what life is about, orders and directs the various types of wealth, and judges their proper use.

IV. Clarifying the Priority of Religious Capital

Objection:

Religious capital does not produce obvious earthly advantage; therefore it seems least useful.

Response:

Precisely because it does not serve mere earthly advantage, religious capital governs the others. It answers the prior question: “What is life for?” Without this ordering principle, economic, social, cultural, and symbolic capital become disordered, dangerous, or vain.

V. Practical Considerations for Building an Inheritance

A. Economic

  • Do we own productive assets?
  • Are we reducing debt?
  • Do my children understand money?

B. Social

  • Do we maintain relationships with grandparents, uncles, neighbors?
  • Will these relations come to the aid of my children?
  • Is our family trusted?

C. Cultural

  • What books will my children know?
  • What traditions will they recognize?
  • What stories will they tell about our family?

D. Symbolic

  • Do they have family pictures or heirlooms?

E. Religious

  • Do my children see me pray?
  • Do they know what our family believes?
  • Do they have a moral framework?
  • Do they see me keep my word?

VI. Conclusion: A Good Inheritance Is a Well-Ordered Life

A father’s inheritance is not primarily wealth but formation. It is the transmission of a rightly ordered life—economic stability, social rootedness, cultural literacy, symbolic identity, and religious conviction woven together into a coherent patrimony for his children and their children after them.
